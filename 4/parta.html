Section A.2

Recover Homographies

A homography \(\mathbf{H}\) is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with 8 degrees of freedom that maps homogeneous coordinates from one planar projection to another: \(\mathbf{p}' \sim \mathbf{H}\mathbf{p}\). Setting \(h_{22} = 1\) acts as our scaling normalization:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} h_{00} & h_{01} & h_{02} \\ h_{10} & h_{11} & h_{12} \\ h_{20} & h_{21} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Expanding this relation yields two linear constraints per point match \((x_i, y_i) \to (x'_i, y'_i)\):

$$x'_i = \frac{h_{00}x_i + h_{01}y_i + h_{02}}{h_{20}x_i + h_{21}y_i + 1} \implies h_{00}x_i + h_{01}y_i + h_{02} - h_{20}x_i x'_i - h_{21}y_i x'_i = x'_i$$ $$y'_i = \frac{h_{10}x_i + h_{11}y_i + h_{12}}{h_{20}x_i + h_{21}y_i + 1} \implies h_{10}x_i + h_{11}y_i + h_{12} - h_{20}x_i y'_i - h_{21}y_i y'_i = y'_i$$

Stacking \(n \ge 4\) correspondences produces an overdetermined system \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b}\), which we solve using least-squares via np.linalg.lstsq to minimize sensitivity to manual clicking noise:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_i & y_i & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_i x'_i & -y_i x'_i \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_i & y_i & 1 & -x_i y'_i & -y_i y'_i \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_{00} \\ h_{01} \\ h_{02} \\ h_{10} \\ h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ h_{20} \\ h_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x'_i \\ y'_i \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

Section A.3

Warp the Images & Rectification

Using forward warping creates sampling holes due to rounding. We instead employ inverse warping. For each discrete pixel \((x', y')\) on the target canvas, we compute its continuous source coordinate \(\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{p}'\) and sample color values using two custom interpolation methods written from scratch.

Nearest Neighbor Interpolation

Selects the nearest pixel to fill each position. It is fast but can make edges look jagged or blocky.

Bilinear Interpolation

Blends the four nearest pixels based on their distances. It produces smoother edges and more natural-looking images, but takes slightly longer to compute.

Runtime Benchmarks

Execution times for inverse warping each target canvas. Nearest-neighbor rounding avoids weighted averaging calculations, whereas bilinear interpolation requires four neighbor lookups and fractional distance weighting per pixel.

Section A.4

Blend Images into a Mosaic

To mosaic the aligned image pairs without harsh boundary cutoffs or double-exposure ghosting, we implement distance-weighted alpha feathering:

  • Canvas Sizing: Project the 4 boundary corners of the secondary frame through \(\mathbf{H}\) to calculate the unified bounding box and origin translation offsets.
  • Warping: Inverse warp the second image into the reference coordinate frame using bilinear interpolation.
  • Alpha Feathering: Compute the exact Euclidean distance transform (scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt) on the binary valid masks of both frames. Blending weights ramp down smoothly near image edges, eliminating seam artifacts across overlapping regions.

Key Takeaways

Solving the homography via an overdetermined least-squares system provides stability against minor human clicking errors. Inverse warping coupled with bilinear interpolation eliminates spatial holes while maintaining sharp edge fidelity. Finally, weight falloff via Euclidean distance transforms prevents exposure steps and seam lines at boundaries.

Putting these pieces together showed me how small technical choices can make a big difference in the final mosaic, and how a few photos taken from different angles can come together to create something much bigger!
